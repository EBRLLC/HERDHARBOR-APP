How to Track Litter Performance and Identify Your Best Breeding Lines

Published October 7, 2026 · Intermediate · About 3 min read

Short version: The best line is not the one that produced your single favorite rabbit. Track whole-litter results across repeated matings and generations: conception, live births, survival, growth, consistency, maternal ability, health, and the percentage of offspring that actually meet your breeding objective.

Start with the mating, not the weaned litter

Litter performance begins with whether the mating resulted in a pregnancy. Record breeding dates, the pair, pregnancy outcome when known, kindling date, and any rebreeding. Repeated missed breedings are part of the reproductive record even though no litter was born.

Record birth outcomes consistently

For each litter, record total born, born alive, stillborn where known, and any early losses. If kits are fostered, preserve the biological parentage and record the foster move separately so maternal performance and genetic parentage do not become mixed.

Measure survival and growth

Survival to weaning is a useful combined outcome influenced by the kits, the doe, litter size, environment, and management. If your program tracks weights, use consistent ages and methods so comparisons are meaningful.

Do not compare raw weights without context when litter size, foster status, illness, age, or management differed substantially.

Evaluate litter uniformity

A litter with one outstanding kit and several poor ones may tell a different genetic story from a litter that is consistently good. Record how many offspring meet your minimum standard and how many display the strengths the mating was designed to produce.

Track maternal performance separately

The doe contributes genetics and the maternal environment. Record nest quality, nursing success, ability to maintain body condition, kit survival, and any repeated management problem. That makes it easier to distinguish a strong genetic litter from a litter that survived only through intensive intervention.

Follow offspring after they leave the nest

Weaning is not the end of evaluation. Structural problems, temperament, adult size, show traits, reproductive performance, and health can become visible later. When possible, link adult offspring results back to the litter and parents.

Compare lines over multiple litters

One litter is a small sample. Look for repeated patterns across different mates and generations. A buck that produces strong growth with several unrelated does provides stronger evidence than a buck judged from one exceptional litter. The same is true for maternal lines.

Useful litter-level metrics

  • Conception rate and successful kindlings.
  • Total born and born alive.
  • Survival to a consistent age or weaning.
  • Average and range of weights at defined ages, if tracked.
  • Percentage of offspring meeting structural or breed goals.
  • Health problems or recurring defects.
  • Number retained as breeding prospects.
  • Later performance of retained offspring.

Bottom line

The best breeding lines reveal themselves through repeated outcomes, not reputation. Record entire litters, connect those results to the parents, and compare families over time. That is how a breeder learns which lines reliably move the program forward.
