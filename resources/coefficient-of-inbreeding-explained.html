Understanding COI: Coefficient of Inbreeding Without the Math Headache

Published October 7, 2026 · Intermediate · About 3 min read

Short version: COI estimates the probability that the two copies of a gene in an animal are identical by descent from a shared ancestor. A higher COI means more pedigree-based homozygosity is expected. It is a useful risk and relationship measure—not a score for animal quality.

What COI is measuring

The coefficient of inbreeding, usually written F or COI, uses pedigree relationships to estimate how likely an offspring is to receive two alleles that trace back to the same ancestral copy. It is a probability based on pedigree structure, not a DNA test of every gene in the animal.

In practical terms, as COI increases, expected homozygosity from shared ancestry increases.

Why inbreeding changes risk

Related animals are more likely to carry the same allele inherited from a common ancestor. When both parents pass that same ancestral allele to an offspring, the offspring becomes homozygous at that location. That can make desired traits more uniform, but it can also expose harmful recessive alleles and contribute to inbreeding depression.

A familiar example

MU Extension shows that, when the parents themselves are not inbred, offspring from a full-sibling mating have an inbreeding coefficient of 25%. The exact result in more complex pedigrees depends on every common ancestor and each path through which that ancestor appears.

Pedigree depth matters

A COI calculated from three generations does not necessarily equal one calculated from ten generations. Deeper pedigrees can reveal additional common ancestors that raise the result. Missing ancestors are usually treated as unrelated founders, which can make the calculated number lower than the animal’s true historical relatedness.

That is why two COI numbers are most comparable when they use similar pedigree depth and data quality.

The same COI can come from different pedigrees

A percentage summarizes relationship but does not tell the whole story. Two animals can have similar COI values produced by different patterns—one may have a recent close mating, while another accumulated many distant repeated ancestors. The management and genetic implications can differ.

There is no universal “safe” number

A COI threshold cannot decide whether a mating is good. Species, population size, pedigree depth, existing genetic diversity, health history, and the rate at which inbreeding is accumulating all matter. A low COI does not rescue a poor mating, and a higher COI does not automatically make an animal defective.

Use the number as one piece of evidence alongside performance, health, family history, and the breeding objective.

How breeders should use COI

  1. Calculate COI before the mating, not only after the litter exists.
  2. Compare proposed pairings using the same pedigree depth.
  3. Look at which ancestors are creating the relatedness, not only the final percentage.
  4. Review health, fertility, growth, and defects in those repeated families.
  5. Track how average relatedness changes across generations.
  6. Use outcrosses strategically when the program is becoming too genetically narrow.

COI is not a quality score

A rabbit, goat, sheep, cow, pig, or poultry line can have a low COI and still carry serious faults. A higher-COI animal can be healthy and productive. COI tells you about pedigree-based relatedness; it does not evaluate structure, temperament, fertility, disease status, or whether the traits being concentrated are desirable.

Bottom line

Use COI to understand how much shared ancestry a planned mating is likely to concentrate. Then go back to the pedigree and the records to understand what that shared ancestry actually represents. The percentage is the start of the question, not the answer.
