Understanding COI: Coefficient of Inbreeding Explained Without the Math Headache

Published October 7, 2026 · 3 min read · Intermediate

In this guide: COI estimates how much of an animal's genome may be homozygous because both copies trace back to the same ancestor. It is a relationship tool—not a score for whether an animal is good or bad.

What COI means

The coefficient of inbreeding, often shortened to COI, is an estimate of the probability that the two alleles at a locus are identical by descent from a common ancestor.

In plain language: it estimates how much shared ancestry has a chance to place the same ancestral allele on both sides of an offspring's genome.

Why breeders use it

A pedigree can become visually complicated very quickly.

COI converts the multiple paths of shared ancestry into a numerical measure that can help compare planned matings.

It is especially useful when two animals share several ancestors at different depths and a simple label such as "cousins" misses part of the relationship.

COI is not a quality score

A low COI does not prove a mating is good.

Two unrelated animals can share the same structural weakness, carry the same harmful allele by chance or population history, or simply be poor representatives of the breeding goal.

A high COI does not prove an animal will be defective.

COI describes relatedness-derived homozygosity risk. It does not list which alleles became homozygous.

Why increasing COI changes risk

Inbreeding increases homozygosity. That can increase consistency for desired genes, but it also makes it more likely that recessive alleles inherited from a shared ancestor will pair together.

Across livestock populations, increased inbreeding has been associated with reduced performance in some traits, especially those connected with reproductive fitness and survival.

The effect is not identical for every species, population, or trait.

Pedigree completeness matters

COI calculated from an incomplete pedigree can underestimate true relatedness.

If unknown ancestors are treated as unrelated founders, the calculation has no way to recognize relationships that existed before the recorded pedigree.

That is why a COI should be interpreted along with:

  • number of known generations,
  • quality of parentage data,
  • size of the breeding population,
  • repeated use of popular sires or dams.

The same COI can come from different pedigrees

Two animals can have similar calculated COI values while reaching them through different family structures.

One pedigree may contain a close recent mating. Another may accumulate many distant repeated ancestors.

The number is useful, but the pedigree pattern behind the number still matters.

Linebreeding and COI

Linebreeding deliberately maintains relationship to a chosen ancestor or family. Because linebreeding is a form of inbreeding in the genetic sense, it can increase COI.

The responsible question is not, "Can I linebreed without inbreeding?" It is, "Is the increased relationship serving a documented breeding goal, and what risks are being monitored?"

Do not chase a universal cutoff

Different breeds and populations have different genetic histories. A universal statement that every mating below one percentage is safe and every mating above it is bad is too simplistic.

Use COI comparatively:

  • Which of several suitable matings adds less relatedness?
  • How quickly is the herd or rabbitry becoming more related over generations?
  • Are fertility or performance trends changing as relatedness rises?
  • Is one ancestor becoming overrepresented?

What COI cannot tell you

COI does not tell you:

  • whether a specific harmful allele is present,
  • whether the animals are structurally sound,
  • whether they are fertile,
  • whether their temperament fits the program,
  • whether the pairing is genetically complementary,
  • whether the pedigree itself is correct.

Practical use

Use COI as one column in the breeding decision, alongside:

  • health,
  • performance,
  • pedigree,
  • family history,
  • genetic testing where relevant,
  • breeding goal,
  • expected strengths and weaknesses.

Bottom line

COI is most useful when it stops a breeder from guessing about relatedness. It is least useful when it becomes a single number used to approve or reject a mating without looking at the animals and the family behind it.
