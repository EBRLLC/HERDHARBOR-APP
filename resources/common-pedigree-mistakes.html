Common Pedigree Mistakes—and How They Affect a Breeding Program

Published October 7, 2026 · 3 min read · Beginner

In this guide: Pedigree errors do more than make a document look sloppy. They can distort relationship calculations, genetic assumptions, ownership history, and future breeding decisions.

Wrong parentage

The most serious pedigree error is assigning the wrong sire or dam.

Every ancestor above that parent then becomes wrong too. Relationship calculations, COI, color/genetic inference, and family-performance analysis can all be affected.

When parentage is uncertain, record uncertainty instead of choosing the most convenient parent.

Duplicate animals

The same animal may be entered twice because of:

  • spelling differences,
  • a changed prefix,
  • nickname versus registered name,
  • imported records,
  • different capitalization,
  • missing permanent ID.

Duplicates can make related animals appear unrelated because software treats the two entries as different founders.

Use a stable unique identifier behind the display name.

Name changes without identity history

Animals sometimes change names after sale or registration.

The system should preserve aliases or prior names without creating a new animal. A name is a label; identity is the underlying record.

Inconsistent dates

Birth dates, breeding dates, and ownership dates can expose data errors.

Examples:

  • a parent recorded as younger than its offspring,
  • a litter born before the breeding date,
  • an animal shown as producing offspring after a recorded death date.

Validation rules should flag impossible timelines without automatically deleting data.

Color recorded as genotype

"Black" is a phenotype. It is not a complete genotype.

Recording a visible color as if every underlying allele were proven creates false confidence in breeding predictions.

Store phenotype and genotype separately.

Missing ancestors treated as unrelated certainty

An unknown pedigree position is unknown—not proof of an unrelated founder.

Relationship calculations that treat every blank ancestor as unrelated can underestimate actual relatedness, especially in small populations.

The result should be accompanied by pedigree-completeness context.

Copying old pedigrees without verification

Historical pedigrees are valuable, but copying them can also copy old mistakes.

Preserve the source of imported pedigree data when possible. If an error is later corrected, record the correction rather than silently rewriting history.

Mixing animals from different accounts

In multi-user software, one breeder's private animal record should not silently become another breeder's editable record merely because the names match.

Shared pedigree identity and account ownership need clear rules. Public pedigree information can be referenced without exposing private account data.

Deleting animals that still exist in pedigrees

Removing a sold, deceased, or retired animal from active management should not destroy its pedigree role.

Archive status and pedigree identity are different concepts.

Overwriting canonical pedigree snapshots

If a pedigree is attached to a listing, certificate, or historical breeding event, the snapshot may need to remain stable even if the live animal record changes later.

A corrected live record and an historical snapshot serve different purposes.

No correction history

People make data-entry mistakes.

A trustworthy breeding record should allow correction while preserving enough audit information to understand what changed, especially for parentage and permanent identity.

Bottom line

Pedigree quality depends on identity discipline. Stable IDs, correct parentage, duplicate prevention, clear uncertainty, and preserved history matter more than a perfectly formatted printable page.
