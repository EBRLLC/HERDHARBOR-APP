Genotype vs. Phenotype: Why an Animal Can Carry Traits You Can’t See

Published October 7, 2026 · 3 min read · Beginner

In this guide: Phenotype is what you observe. Genotype is the genetic information behind the animal. Understanding the difference explains hidden recessives, unexpected offspring, and why appearance alone cannot predict every mating.

Phenotype

Phenotype is an observable or measurable characteristic.

Examples include:

  • coat color,
  • horn status,
  • body weight,
  • growth rate,
  • conformation,
  • milk production,
  • litter size,
  • temperament measurements.

Some phenotypes are strongly influenced by one gene. Others reflect many genes plus environment.

Genotype

Genotype describes the alleles an animal carries at a gene or across its genome.

An animal can carry a recessive allele without expressing the recessive phenotype. That is why two animals that look alike can produce different offspring.

A simple recessive example

Assume B is dominant and b is recessive.

  • BB shows the dominant phenotype and does not carry b.
  • Bb shows the dominant phenotype but carries b.
  • bb shows the recessive phenotype.

The BB and Bb animals may look the same at that trait, but they are not genetically identical.

Carriers are normal genetics

"Carrier" is not automatically a disease label.

It simply means an animal carries an allele that may not be expressed in its own phenotype. In disease genetics, carrier status can be important for avoiding affected offspring. In coat-color genetics, it may simply explain a hidden color.

Context matters.

Phenotype can be influenced by environment

Many important traits are quantitative.

Growth, body weight, fertility, production, and other performance traits can be influenced by genetics, nutrition, disease, management, season, age, and other environmental factors.

A poor phenotype does not always prove poor genetics, and a strong phenotype under exceptional management does not prove every offspring will perform the same way.

Epistasis can hide genetic information

Genes do not always act independently.

One gene can modify or mask the visible effect of another. Rabbit coat color provides easy examples: an animal can carry alleles for base color and pattern that are not visually obvious because another locus changes pigment expression.

How breeders learn genotype

Possible evidence includes:

  • phenotype,
  • parents,
  • offspring,
  • pedigree,
  • validated DNA testing.

Each source has a different level of certainty.

A recessive offspring can sometimes prove that a phenotypically dominant parent carried the recessive allele. Conversely, producing no recessive offspring in a small sample does not prove the allele is absent.

Do not convert assumptions into facts

Record:

  • known,
  • inferred,
  • possible,
  • unknown,
  • laboratory confirmed.

This is especially important in software. A breeding calculator becomes misleading if an unproven genotype is silently treated as certain.

The breeding lesson

Choose animals using more than phenotype.

For visible Mendelian traits, genotype may help predict offspring. For complex traits, family and performance records become more important.

The best breeding decisions combine what the animal looks like, what the genetics can support, and what the family has actually produced.

Bottom line

Phenotype answers, "What do I observe?" Genotype answers, "What genetic information is behind it?" Breeders need both—and they need to be honest about what is still unknown.
