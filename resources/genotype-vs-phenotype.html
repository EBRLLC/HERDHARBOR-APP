Genotype vs. Phenotype: Why an Animal Can Carry Traits You Cannot See

Published October 7, 2026 · Beginner · About 3 min read

Short version: Genotype is the genetic information an animal carries; phenotype is the trait you can observe or measure. Dominance, gene interactions, many-gene traits, and environment can all create a gap between the two, which is why breeders should not treat appearance as complete genetic proof.

Genotype: the inherited information

For a particular gene, genotype describes the alleles an animal carries. In a simple autosomal example, an animal may inherit one allele from the sire and one from the dam. Those two copies may be the same or different.

Phenotype: what you can observe

Phenotype includes visible traits such as color and pattern as well as measurable traits such as body weight, growth, fertility, milk production, litter size, or disease resistance. Phenotype is produced by genetics interacting with other genes and, for many traits, the environment.

Why a recessive allele can be hidden

When one allele is dominant over another, a heterozygous animal may show the dominant phenotype while carrying the recessive allele. The recessive trait can reappear in offspring if the other parent also contributes the appropriate recessive allele.

Rabbit black-versus-chocolate base color is a straightforward teaching example: a black-based rabbit may carry a recessive chocolate allele without looking chocolate.

Gene interactions can hide even more

One gene can alter or mask the expression of another gene. This is called epistasis. In rabbits, the albino genotype at the C locus can prevent other coat-color genotypes from being visible, even though those alleles are still present and can be passed to offspring.

Many important traits are not simple dominant/recessive traits

Growth, fertility, maternal performance, longevity, body type, milk production, and many other production traits are influenced by many genes plus environment. For these traits, breeders learn more from repeated records, relatives, and offspring than from a single Punnett square.

Environment changes phenotype too

Nutrition, disease, climate, age, litter size, housing, and management can all affect how a genetic potential is expressed. Two genetically similar animals raised under different conditions can perform differently. That is why fair comparison requires consistent management and context.

How to strengthen a genotype assumption

  1. Start with the phenotype but do not over-interpret it.
  2. Use known parent genotypes and accurate pedigree information.
  3. Use offspring outcomes to reveal hidden alleles when inheritance is well understood.
  4. Use validated genetic testing when available and appropriate.
  5. Mark unknowns rather than forcing certainty.
  6. For complex traits, use performance records and family data instead of pretending a simple genotype explains everything.

Bottom line

Appearance is evidence, not a complete genetic readout. Strong breeding records separate what is seen from what is genetically known and allow certainty to increase as parentage, offspring, tests, and performance provide more information.
