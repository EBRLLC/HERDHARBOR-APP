What Makes a Good Breeding Record?

Published October 7, 2026 · 3 min read · Beginner

In this guide: A good breeding record preserves the full reproductive history: what was attempted, when it happened, what the result was, and what the breeder learned.

The breeding event

Record:

  • female,
  • male,
  • date,
  • method if relevant,
  • repeat services/matings,
  • notes that could affect interpretation.

Do this immediately. Reconstructing dates from memory later creates avoidable errors.

Expected timing

Where gestation or incubation timing is predictable, calculate and store the expected date or window.

The expected date should be generated from the actual breeding/service date and species-specific rules—not typed independently when it can be calculated.

Pregnancy or fertility checks

If the species or management program uses pregnancy diagnosis, candling, palpation, ultrasound, return-to-estrus checks, or other confirmation, record:

  • date,
  • method,
  • result,
  • who performed it when relevant.

Do not convert "not confirmed" into "not pregnant" unless the method supports that conclusion.

Birth or hatch outcome

Record the complete outcome.

Possible fields:

  • actual date,
  • total born/hatch count,
  • live born,
  • stillborn,
  • viable hatchlings,
  • abnormalities,
  • sex when known and useful,
  • assisted delivery,
  • losses,
  • fostered/transferred young.

The exact fields vary by species, but the principle is the same.

Link the offspring

Once individual offspring records are created, they should link back to the breeding event and parents automatically.

That prevents the litter record and pedigree from becoming two independent versions of the truth.

Record failures

A breeding attempt that does not result in offspring still belongs in the history.

Record:

  • no conception,
  • return to estrus,
  • suspected loss,
  • confirmed loss,
  • infertile eggs,
  • abandoned breeding,
  • unknown outcome.

Use the most accurate status available without inventing certainty.

Complications

Record reproductive problems separately from routine notes.

Examples:

  • dystocia,
  • retained placenta where applicable,
  • pregnancy toxemia or metabolic complications where applicable,
  • mastitis,
  • poor maternal behavior,
  • neonatal losses,
  • veterinary intervention.

These events may influence whether a mating is repeated or an animal remains in the breeding program.

Reproductive intervals

Over time, calculate useful trends such as:

  • age at first breeding,
  • conception rate,
  • interval between offspring groups,
  • average litter or clutch outcome,
  • offspring survival,
  • repeated failure rate.

A single event is a story. A series of events is data.

Keep raw events and summaries

Do not replace detailed history with an average.

The system should store individual events, then calculate summaries from them. That way a breeder can see both "80% conception rate" and the five attempts that produced that number.

Bottom line

A breeding record is good when another person can read it later and understand exactly what happened without asking the breeder to remember. Complete reproductive history is one of the strongest tools for improving a breeding program.
