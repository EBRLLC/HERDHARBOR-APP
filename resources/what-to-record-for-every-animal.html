What to Record for Every Animal in a Breeding Program

Published October 7, 2026 · 3 min read · Beginner

In this guide: Good records turn individual animals into usable breeding information. The goal is not to collect every possible detail—it is to preserve the information that changes decisions.

Identity comes first

Every record should start with a permanent identity.

At minimum, keep:

  • unique ID,
  • name or call name if used,
  • species,
  • breed,
  • sex,
  • birth or hatch date when known,
  • breeder or source,
  • current ownership/status,
  • permanent identification such as ear number, tag, tattoo, registration number, microchip, or flock/herd ID where applicable.

Duplicate animals created under slightly different names are a major source of pedigree and breeding errors.

Parentage and pedigree

Record sire and dam whenever they are known. Preserve the original identity of those parents even if their display names change later.

Pedigree data becomes more useful when the same ancestor is represented by one canonical record rather than several near-duplicates.

Physical and production information

The exact measurements depend on species and breeding goal.

Possible records include:

  • birth weight,
  • weaning weight,
  • mature weight,
  • growth rate,
  • body condition,
  • fleece/fiber information,
  • egg production,
  • milk or production records,
  • conformation scores,
  • show results,
  • carcass or performance data where appropriate.

Record the date and context of measurements. A weight with no age or date has much less value.

Reproductive history

Keep both successes and failures.

Record:

  • mating or service date,
  • mate,
  • breeding method where relevant,
  • pregnancy confirmation if used,
  • expected due/hatch date,
  • actual birth/hatch date,
  • number born or hatched,
  • number alive,
  • weaning outcome,
  • complications,
  • rebreeding interval,
  • failed breeding or pregnancy loss.

Failed attempts are data. Deleting them creates a falsely perfect reproductive history.

Health records

Health records should include:

  • problem or observation,
  • date,
  • diagnosis when one is established,
  • veterinary visit,
  • treatment,
  • medication/product,
  • dose and route as directed,
  • treatment duration,
  • response,
  • testing results,
  • vaccination records where applicable,
  • withdrawal information for food-producing animals when relevant,
  • recurring problems.

Do not rewrite an old record because the diagnosis later changed. Add the corrected information and preserve the chronology.

Genetics

Separate four levels of certainty:

  • observed phenotype,
  • inferred genotype,
  • breeder-reported genotype,
  • laboratory-confirmed result.

Those are not equivalent.

If an offspring proves a recessive allele is present in a parent, update the parent's genetic evidence while preserving how that conclusion was reached.

Photos

Photos can document development, markings, identification, condition, structure, and changes over time.

Date them. A current photo should not silently replace every historical image if comparison has breeding or health value.

Disposition

When an animal leaves the active breeding group, record why:

  • sold,
  • transferred,
  • retained as nonbreeding,
  • retired,
  • culled from the breeding program,
  • deceased.

For breeding improvement, the reason matters. "Removed" tells you nothing. "Retired after declining fertility" or "not retained because of recurring structural weakness" is usable information.

Keep records consistent

A breeding program becomes difficult to analyze when one animal has detailed records and another has only a name.

Use the same core fields for every animal, then add species-specific fields when they matter.

Bottom line

The most valuable record is the one that lets you compare animals and generations honestly. Record identity, parentage, reproduction, health, performance, and the reasons behind major breeding decisions. Over time, those records become more useful than memory or pedigree reputation.
