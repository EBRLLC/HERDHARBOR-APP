Rabbit Genetics: From Genes to Breeding Decisions

Published October 2, 2026 · Educational reference

Start here: genetics is the study of how biological information is inherited. Every rabbit receives genetic material from both parents. Some traits can be predicted with relatively simple inheritance rules, while others—such as mature size, fertility, growth, litter performance, and overall body type—are influenced by many genes plus environment. A pedigree tells you where genes may have come from; records tell you what those genes actually produced.

1. DNA, chromosomes, genes, and alleles

A rabbit's body is built and maintained using instructions encoded in DNA. DNA is organized into chromosomes. Genes are specific sections of DNA associated with biological functions or traits, and different versions of the same gene are called alleles.

For most autosomal genes, a rabbit has two copies—one inherited from its sire and one from its dam. Those two alleles together form the rabbit's genotype at that gene. What you can observe is part of the rabbit's phenotype.

That distinction matters because phenotype does not always reveal genotype. A rabbit may look black, for example, while carrying a recessive chocolate allele that is invisible until it is paired with another rabbit carrying the same recessive allele.

2. Genotype vs. phenotype

TermMeaningRabbit example
GenotypeThe alleles a rabbit carries.A rabbit may be Bb at the B locus.
PhenotypeThe observable result of genes interacting with one another and, for many traits, the environment.A Bb rabbit can show a black base while silently carrying chocolate.
CarrierAn animal that carries a recessive allele without expressing the recessive phenotype.A black-based rabbit carrying b can pass chocolate to offspring.

A breeder should never assume that two rabbits with the same appearance have the same genotype. Pedigrees, known parents, previous offspring, and genetic testing where available can provide additional information.

3. Dominant and recessive inheritance

In a simple dominant/recessive relationship, a dominant allele can affect phenotype when only one copy is present. A recessive allele generally needs two copies before its effect is visible.

Using the rabbit B locus as a simple example, B is dominant and produces a black base, while b is recessive and produces a chocolate base when the rabbit is bb.

GenotypeWhat it means
BBBlack-base allele from both parents; does not carry chocolate at this locus.
BbBlack-based phenotype but carries the recessive chocolate allele.
bbChocolate-based phenotype.

Dominant does not mean “better,” “stronger,” “more common,” or “healthier.” It only describes how an allele is expressed relative to another allele at that locus.

4. Homozygous and heterozygous

Homozygous means the two alleles at a locus are the same, such as BB or bb. Heterozygous means they differ, such as Bb.

This matters because heterozygous rabbits can carry recessive alleles that are not obvious from appearance. A breeding program that records only color names and not known or suspected genotype will eventually encounter surprises.

5. Using a Punnett square

A Punnett square is a probability tool. It shows the possible allele combinations offspring can inherit from two parents. It does not guarantee that a litter of four will contain one rabbit of each predicted category.

If two black-based rabbits are both known carriers of chocolate—Bb × Bb—the expected genotype probabilities for each kit are:

B from parentb from parent
B from parentBBBb
b from parentBbbb

For each offspring, the expected probability is 25% BB, 50% Bb, and 25% bb. Phenotypically, that is an expected 75% black-based and 25% chocolate-based at this locus, assuming other color genes do not mask or alter the result.

Probability is not a litter recipe: every conception is a new genetic event. A four-kit litter could contain zero chocolate kits, one, two, three, or four even when the calculated probability is 25% per kit.

6. Why rabbit color genetics gets more complicated

A single-gene Punnett square is useful for learning, but coat color is created by multiple genes working together. Oregon State University Extension describes a teaching framework involving the A, B, C, D, and E genes plus spotting and silvering, while noting that rabbit coat genetics includes additional complexity and some genetic mechanisms remain incompletely characterized.

Genes can modify or mask the effects of other genes. This interaction is called epistasis. That is why knowing one locus does not always tell you what the rabbit will look like.

8. The A locus: agouti, tan pattern, and self

The A locus affects how pigment is arranged in the coat. In the commonly taught dominance order, A (agouti) is dominant to at (tan pattern), which is dominant to a (self).

An agouti-pattern rabbit can therefore carry tan-pattern or self alleles without showing them. A tan-pattern rabbit can carry self. A self rabbit must have two self alleles at this locus.

This is an example of a locus with more than two alleles. Breeders sometimes oversimplify genetics into “dominant or recessive,” but many loci have an ordered series of alleles.

9. The B locus: black vs. chocolate base

The B locus is one of the clearest examples for beginners. A rabbit with at least one dominant B allele has a black pigment base. A rabbit that is bb has a chocolate pigment base.

Other loci can change how that base appears. A dilute black becomes blue under dd at the D locus, while a dilute chocolate becomes lilac. Other genes may change pattern, pigment distribution, or how much dark pigment is expressed.

10. The C locus: full color, chinchilla series, Himalayan, and albino

The C locus affects pigment production and expression. Oregon State University's summary lists a dominance series beginning with full color and progressing through chinchilla-related alleles, Himalayan, and albino.

The key concept is that cc is epistatic to other coat-color genotypes: an albino rabbit may genetically carry alleles for black, chocolate, agouti, self, dilute, and other color traits even though those traits are not visible in the coat.

This is a powerful example of why phenotype alone cannot reveal everything a rabbit can produce.

11. The D locus: dense vs. dilute

The dominant D allele allows dense pigment. The recessive dilute phenotype requires dd. Dilution changes the visual result of the base pigment: black can become blue and chocolate can become lilac.

A dense-colored rabbit may be DD or Dd. Only breeding history, parentage, offspring, or genetic testing can distinguish those possibilities with confidence.

12. The E locus: extension and pattern interaction

The E locus influences the extension of dark pigment in the hair. It is one reason rabbit color genetics becomes much more complex than a single black-versus-chocolate example.

Some E-locus combinations can alter or override what you might otherwise expect from the B locus. For example, Oregon State University describes the recessive non-extension genotype ee as producing red/orange/fawn-type expression rather than allowing black or brown pigment to extend normally over the back.

This interaction between loci is epistasis: one genotype changes how another genotype is expressed.

13. Broken pattern and spotting

The KIT-associated spotting locus contributes to the typical broken pattern. Oregon State University describes Enen as the usual heterozygous genotype for broken spotting, enen as no spotting from this locus, and EnEn as an almost-all-white phenotype often called a “Charlie.”

Pattern inheritance should never be reduced to “breed two brokens and get more brokens.” The genotype of each parent matters, and welfare concerns associated with particular genetic combinations should be understood before intentionally producing them.

14. What an underscore means in genotype notation

Rabbit breeders often write an underscore when the second allele is unknown. For example, B_ means the rabbit has at least one B allele because its phenotype proves that much, but the breeder does not yet know whether the rabbit is BB or Bb.

Likewise, an agouti rabbit might be written A_ when the second allele has not been identified. Good genotype notation separates what is known from what is only assumed.

15. Hidden recessives and “surprise” kits

A recessive allele can pass silently through multiple generations. Suppose a buck and doe both look black-based but are each Bb. Neither parent is chocolate, yet each can pass b. If the same kit receives b from both parents, that kit is bb and can express chocolate.

The chocolate kit did not appear because a new gene suddenly formed. The recessive alleles were already present in the parents.

This is why recording unexpected offspring is valuable. Offspring can reveal hidden genotype information about both parents.

16. Pedigrees are genetic maps—not genetic guarantees

A pedigree shows ancestry. It can tell you which rabbits may have contributed alleles to a descendant and whether particular ancestors repeat. It cannot prove every allele carried by a rabbit unless that information is supported by phenotype, offspring, parentage, or testing.

For breeding decisions, a useful pedigree should be paired with records of health, fertility, litter performance, growth, structural strengths and weaknesses, temperament, and the traits actually produced by previous matings.

17. Not every important trait follows a simple Punnett square

Color genetics is popular because several coat traits can be modeled with identifiable loci and recognizable allele relationships. Many economically or functionally important traits are much more complex.

Traits such as mature body size, growth rate, feed efficiency, fertility, litter size, milk production, maternal performance, longevity, and overall conformation are generally influenced by many genes. These are often called polygenic or quantitative traits.

Environment also matters. Nutrition, disease, housing, temperature, age, management, and maternal effects can change phenotype even when the underlying genetics are the same.

Practical rule: use Punnett squares for clearly defined allele relationships. Use multi-generation performance records for complex traits.

18. What heritability actually means

Heritability is often misunderstood. It does not mean that a certain percentage of an individual rabbit's trait “came from genetics.” Heritability is a population-level statistic describing how much of the observed variation in a trait, under a particular set of conditions, is associated with genetic differences among individuals.

A trait with lower heritability can still be genetically influenced; it may simply be strongly affected by environment. For breeders, this means selection tends to be more predictable for some traits than others, and accurate records across relatives and generations become especially important for complex traits.

19. Genetic selection: what you keep changes the next generation

Every breeding decision changes which alleles are more likely to be represented in the next generation. Selection is therefore not only choosing which rabbits mate; it is choosing which offspring remain in the breeding population.

If a breeder repeatedly selects for one visible trait while ignoring fertility, health, temperament, or structural soundness, the herd may improve in the visible trait while losing ground elsewhere. Utah State University Extension emphasizes that successful breeding programs usually involve multiple traits rather than a single characteristic.

20. Genes vs. environment

Genetics establishes biological potential, but environment influences how much of that potential is expressed. A rabbit genetically capable of strong growth may perform poorly under inadequate nutrition or disease pressure. Conversely, excellent management cannot permanently breed a genetic trait into a line if the underlying alleles are not present.

This is why breeding records should distinguish inherited patterns from management problems whenever possible.

21. Using offspring to learn a parent's genotype

Test breeding and offspring records can reveal hidden alleles. If a rabbit with a dominant phenotype produces a recessive offspring with a mate known to carry the recessive allele, then the first rabbit must also have contributed that recessive allele.

However, absence of a recessive offspring does not prove a rabbit is genetically clear. A small number of offspring may simply fail to inherit the recessive combination by chance.

22. Why litter size matters when judging probabilities

Genetic ratios are expected probabilities over many offspring, not promises for individual litters. The smaller the sample, the easier it is for results to differ sharply from the expected ratio.

A breeder should avoid rewriting a rabbit's assumed genotype after one small litter unless the offspring provide logically conclusive evidence—for example, the appearance of a recessive phenotype that requires an allele from each parent.

23. Mutation, recombination, and why siblings are different

Full siblings are not genetically identical. During the production of eggs and sperm, chromosomes are shuffled and recombined. Each kit receives a different combination of parental genetic material, except in the rare case of genetically identical multiples.

New mutations can also occur, but most unexpected traits in ordinary breeding programs are better investigated first through known inheritance, hidden recessives, pedigree accuracy, and gene interactions rather than assuming a new mutation.

24. Sex inheritance

Like other typical mammals, female rabbits have two X chromosomes and males have one X and one Y. The doe contributes an X chromosome to every kit, while the buck contributes either X or Y. In genetic terms, the sperm therefore determines chromosomal sex.

Normal litter-to-litter variation can still produce strongly uneven sex ratios by chance. A doe producing several male-heavy or female-heavy litters does not, by itself, prove that she “throws” one sex.

25. Genetic diversity matters

A breeding program needs enough genetic diversity to avoid continuously increasing relatedness and homozygosity without a purpose. Line breeding can be useful when carefully managed, but concentrating a family also concentrates recessive alleles and can contribute to inbreeding depression if relatedness becomes excessive.

For a deeper explanation, see HerdHarbor's companion guide: Line Breeding Rabbits: A Practical Genetics Guide.

26. A practical genetics workflow for breeders

  1. Define the breeding goal. Decide whether you are selecting for color, type, production, temperament, health, or a combination.
  2. Record the phenotype accurately. Do not use vague color names if a more precise breed-standard term exists.
  3. Record known genotype separately from assumed genotype. Use unknown markers rather than pretending certainty.
  4. Study both pedigrees. Look for colors, traits, faults, and repeated ancestors that may reveal hidden alleles or relatedness.
  5. Calculate simple crosses where appropriate. Punnett squares are useful for specific known loci.
  6. Do not use simple Mendelian math for complex performance traits. Use production records and family history.
  7. Record every litter outcome. Unexpected offspring are genetic information.
  8. Update parent genotypes when offspring prove something new.
  9. Select the whole rabbit. Do not sacrifice health, fertility, structure, or temperament for one color or pattern.
  10. Re-evaluate across generations. A breeding program is a long-term population, not a single mating.

27. Common genetics mistakes

“Dominant means more desirable.”

Wrong. Dominance describes expression, not quality.

“If I can't see it, the rabbit doesn't carry it.”

Wrong. Recessive alleles can be hidden in heterozygous animals.

“Two rabbits of the same color have the same genetics.”

Not necessarily. They may carry different recessives or reach a similar phenotype through different combinations.

“A 25% probability means one out of every four kits.”

Not in every litter. It means each conception has that expected probability under the stated genetic assumptions.

“Pedigree color names prove genotype.”

They provide evidence, not complete proof. Misidentification, incomplete pedigrees, and hidden alleles all matter.

“All traits can be predicted with Punnett squares.”

No. Many important breeding traits are polygenic and environmentally influenced.

28. Bottom line

Rabbit genetics becomes much easier when you separate three questions: What does the rabbit look like? What alleles does it actually carry? What has it proven it can produce?

Simple loci can help predict colors and patterns. Pedigrees help trace possible inheritance. Multi-generation records are essential for complex traits. The best breeding decisions use all three rather than relying on appearance alone.

Genetics does not replace selection. It makes selection more informed.
