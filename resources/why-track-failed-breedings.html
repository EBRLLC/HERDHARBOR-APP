Why Breeders Should Track Failed Breedings Too

Published October 7, 2026 · 3 min read · Beginner

In this guide: Failed breedings are not empty records. They can reveal fertility decline, management problems, seasonal effects, pair-specific issues, or health concerns that successful breeding records alone cannot show.

Success-only records create a false history

If only successful pregnancies, litters, clutches, or farrowings are recorded, the system cannot calculate true conception or breeding success.

An animal that produced three offspring groups might have conceived on three attempts—or on nine attempts. Those are very different reproductive histories.

Record every intentional attempt

Capture:

  • date,
  • sire and dam,
  • breeding/service method,
  • repeat service,
  • observed heat/estrus information where relevant,
  • result,
  • how the result was determined.

Use statuses such as:

  • successful,
  • no conception,
  • return to estrus,
  • suspected loss,
  • confirmed pregnancy loss,
  • infertile eggs,
  • incomplete/unknown.

Patterns can reveal animal-level fertility problems

Repeated failures by one female across different males may suggest a female-side issue.

Repeated failures by one male across several proven females may raise concern about the male.

Those patterns are much harder to see when unsuccessful attempts disappear from the record.

Pair-specific outcomes matter

Sometimes two individually fertile animals perform poorly as a specific pairing while succeeding elsewhere.

That does not automatically prove biological incompatibility, but the pattern is worth recording before repeatedly making the same match.

Environment and season can show up in failure data

Heat stress, nutrition, light exposure, disease, housing changes, and other environmental factors can affect reproduction depending on species.

If failures cluster during one season or management change, the problem may not be the animals' genetics.

Failed breeding can be an early warning

A previously reliable breeder that begins missing repeatedly may need:

  • health evaluation,
  • body-condition review,
  • age-related reassessment,
  • semen or reproductive evaluation where appropriate,
  • management changes.

The record can show when the decline began.

Do not overdiagnose from records alone

A failed breeding is an event, not a diagnosis.

Records can identify a pattern that deserves investigation, but they cannot by themselves determine whether the cause is infertility, timing, infection, nutrition, heat stress, embryonic loss, or another factor.

Calculate meaningful rates

Once failed attempts are retained, the program can calculate:

  • attempts per conception,
  • conception rate,
  • repeat-breeding frequency,
  • seasonal success,
  • sire and dam reproductive trends.

Those summaries are useful only if the underlying events are complete.

Use failure data in retirement decisions

A breeder should not be retired for one failed attempt. However, a documented pattern of declining fertility can support a retirement or veterinary-evaluation decision much better than memory.

Bottom line

A failed breeding is still breeding data. Keep it. Over time, the unsuccessful attempts often explain the successful ones and prevent breeders from repeating the same mistakes.
