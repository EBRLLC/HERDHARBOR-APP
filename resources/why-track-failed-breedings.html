Why Breeders Should Track Failed Breedings Too

Published October 7, 2026 · Beginner · About 3 min read

Short version: A failed breeding is not “nothing.” It is a reproductive outcome. When failures are recorded with the same discipline as successful births, breeders can see fertility trends, seasonal effects, pair-specific problems, age-related decline, disease concerns, and management errors that would otherwise stay hidden.

Success-only records distort fertility

If the record begins only when offspring are born, an animal that needed four attempts to conceive can look identical to one that conceived on the first exposure. That hides a major difference in reproductive efficiency.

Record every intentional breeding or exposure, even when no pregnancy results.

Track the type of failure when you can

  • No observed mating or refusal to mate.
  • Mating occurred but no pregnancy detected.
  • Pregnancy suspected or confirmed but no normal birth occurred.
  • Repeat breeding after an expected due date passed.
  • Infertile or non-developing eggs where applicable.
  • Very small or nonviable birth outcome.
  • Male fertility concern across multiple females.

Patterns can identify which animal needs attention

One failed mating can be chance, timing, stress, season, body condition, or another temporary factor. Repeated failures become more informative. If one female misses with several proven males, the pattern differs from one male failing across several proven females.

Pair-level records prevent every failure from being blamed on the same sex by assumption.

Failure can reveal management problems

Heat stress, poor body condition, nutritional problems, disease, incorrect timing, inadequate male exposure, housing issues, and handling stress can all reduce reproductive success depending on the species. A dated breeding record lets you compare failures with environmental or management changes.

Failure can reveal age-related or health trends

Declining conception rate may be one of the first reasons to reassess an older breeding animal. It can also point toward illness, reproductive pathology, or herd-level disease risk. Records do not diagnose the cause, but they tell you when the pattern is real enough to investigate.

Failed breedings improve selection decisions

Fertility is part of breeding value. An animal that consistently requires repeated attempts, produces repeated pregnancy losses, or has poor reproductive efficiency may not belong in a program focused on functional, productive stock—even if it excels in appearance or another secondary trait.

A simple failed-breeding record

  1. Date and both animal IDs.
  2. Observed mating or exposure details.
  3. Pregnancy check result or expected due window.
  4. Outcome: pregnant, open, loss, unknown, or repeat.
  5. Environmental or health notes.
  6. Next action and date.
  7. Whether the pattern changes future breeding or retirement decisions.

Bottom line

The breeding attempts that do not produce offspring are often the records that explain why a reproductive problem is happening. Keep them. A complete fertility history is more valuable than a clean-looking record that contains only successes.
