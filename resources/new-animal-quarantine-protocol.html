Quarantine Protocols When Bringing a New Animal Into Your Herd, Flock, or Rabbitry

Published October 7, 2026 · Beginner · About 3 min read

Short version: New animals can look healthy while carrying infectious disease. A quarantine area should physically separate them from resident animals, use dedicated feed and water equipment, be serviced after the main group, and remain in place long enough for observation and any species-specific testing or veterinary plan.

Why quarantine matters

Introducing new animals is one of the easiest ways to introduce disease into an established group. Incubating or subclinical infections may not be visible on arrival. Isolation buys time to observe the animal before it shares air space, equipment, manure, feed areas, or direct contact with the resident herd, flock, or rabbitry.

How long should quarantine last?

There is no single duration that is correct for every species, disease risk, and operation. Extension guidance commonly recommends roughly two to four weeks for many livestock situations, with some species programs using at least 30 days. The correct period should reflect the species, source, disease risks, testing plan, and veterinarian’s guidance.

For example, Penn State recommends three to four weeks for newly purchased livestock in general guidance, while MU Extension recommends at least 30 days for incoming sheep.

Physical separation matters more than the label

A pen called “quarantine” is not effective if animals can touch noses through the fence, share waterers, swap bedding, or use the same equipment without cleaning. The goal is to interrupt transmission pathways.

  • Use separate feed and water containers.
  • Avoid direct and fence-line contact when feasible.
  • Use dedicated tools, carriers, grooming equipment, and footwear where practical.
  • Do not move manure, bedding, or waste from the quarantine area through clean animal areas unnecessarily.

Work with quarantined animals last

A simple traffic pattern reduces cross-contamination: care for healthy resident animals first, then quarantine animals, then sick animals if a separate isolation area is needed. Wash hands and change or clean footwear and equipment before returning to clean areas.

Observe and record every day

Record appetite, manure, respiratory signs, temperature where appropriate, skin or coat changes, lameness, reproductive signs, and anything abnormal. A written baseline helps you notice change and provides useful information if a veterinarian becomes involved.

Use quarantine for testing and preventative planning

Testing, vaccination, parasite control, and acclimation differ by species, region, source, and operation. Work with a veterinarian or species-specific health program to decide what belongs in the quarantine plan rather than automatically applying the same protocol to every animal.

Returning animals can be a risk too

Animals returning from shows, fairs, breeding visits, boarding, sales, or other farms may have had new exposures. Your biosecurity plan should decide whether and how returning animals are separated instead of treating only purchased animals as a risk.

When to restart or extend quarantine

If an animal becomes ill during quarantine, do not count the original arrival date as proof that the risk period is over. The quarantine plan may need to restart or extend based on the disease concern, testing, and veterinary guidance. Likewise, adding a new arrival to an existing quarantine group can reset the exposure clock for the entire group.

Bottom line

Quarantine is a management system, not a spare pen. Separation, dedicated equipment, clean traffic flow, daily records, and a species-specific health plan work together to protect the animals already on the property.
