Rabbit Color Genetics for Beginners

Published October 7, 2026 · 3 min read · Beginner

In this guide: Rabbit coat color becomes easier when you learn a few basic genetics terms, then study one locus at a time instead of trying to memorize every color combination.

Start with genotype and phenotype

Genotype means the alleles a rabbit carries. Phenotype means what you can observe.

Those two things are related but not identical. A black-based rabbit may carry a recessive chocolate allele without looking chocolate. A rabbit can therefore pass a color gene that is not obvious from its own coat.

Dominant does not mean better

A dominant allele can influence phenotype when one copy is present. A recessive phenotype generally requires the rabbit to inherit a recessive allele from both parents.

Dominant does not mean healthier, stronger, more common, or more desirable. It describes an inheritance relationship.

The basic loci

A practical beginner framework uses several major coat-color loci:

A locus — pattern

The A locus influences agouti, tan-pattern, and self patterning.

B locus — black or chocolate base

The dominant B allele supports a black base. A rabbit with bb has a chocolate base.

C locus — pigment expression

The C series includes full-color and progressively reduced pigment expressions, including Himalayan and albino forms. Some alleles can mask colors that are genetically present elsewhere.

D locus — dense or dilute

A dd rabbit is dilute. A black base can appear blue when dilute; chocolate can appear lilac.

E locus — extension

The E series affects how dark pigment extends through the coat and contributes to patterns such as normal extension, steel, harlequin-type expression, and non-extension colors.

Spotting

The KIT-associated spotting locus contributes to common broken-pattern expression. The genotype matters; "broken" is not simply an independent paint pattern.

Silvering

Silvering is influenced by additional inheritance and can vary in expression.

Why one locus is never the entire color

Rabbit color is produced by genes interacting with one another. A rabbit might genetically have a black base, but other loci can dilute it, change its pattern, reduce pigment, or prevent dark pigment from extending normally.

That gene-to-gene interaction is one reason color calculators need complete and accurate input.

A simple B-locus example

Imagine both parents are Bb.

Each parent can pass either B or b.

Expected genotype probabilities for each kit are:

  • 25% BB
  • 50% Bb
  • 25% bb

At this locus, BB and Bb are black-based while bb is chocolate-based.

That does not mean every four-kit litter must contain one chocolate kit. Each conception is a separate probability event.

What an underscore means

Breeders often use an underscore to represent an unknown second allele.

B_ means the rabbit has at least one B allele, but the second allele has not been proven. The rabbit could be BB or Bb.

Writing the unknown honestly is better than guessing.

Hidden recessives explain many surprises

If two black-based rabbits both carry b, they can produce a chocolate-based kit. The chocolate gene did not suddenly appear. Both parents already carried the recessive allele.

Unexpected offspring are therefore useful records. They can prove something about the parents' genotypes.

Pedigrees provide clues, not certainty

Colors in a pedigree can narrow the possibilities for what an animal carries. However:

  • old pedigrees can contain errors,
  • phenotype can mask recessives,
  • some color names are used inconsistently,
  • not every genotype can be proven visually.

Treat pedigree color information as evidence, not magic.

Do not use color genetics to ignore the whole rabbit

Color can be an enjoyable and useful part of a breeding program, but it should not override health, soundness, temperament, fertility, or breed goals.

A genetically interesting color is not a justification for repeating a poor mating.

Beginner workflow

  1. Identify the rabbit's phenotype accurately.
  2. Record only alleles that are proven or strongly supported.
  3. Mark unknown alleles as unknown.
  4. Review parent and offspring colors.
  5. Work one locus at a time.
  6. Combine loci only after the basic inheritance at each one makes sense.
  7. Update the genotype record when offspring provide new evidence.

Bottom line

Rabbit color genetics is not one giant formula. It is a group of inheritance systems interacting with one another. Learn the vocabulary, keep known and assumed genetics separate, and let pedigrees plus offspring records gradually improve what you know.
