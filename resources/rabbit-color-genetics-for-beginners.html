Rabbit Color Genetics for Beginners: The A, B, C, D, and E Basics

Published October 7, 2026 · Beginner · About 3 min read

Short version: Rabbit color is controlled by several genes working together. The easiest way to begin is to separate phenotype—what the rabbit looks like—from genotype—the alleles it carries—and then learn what the A, B, C, D, and E loci generally influence.

First: phenotype is not the same as genotype

Phenotype is what you can observe, such as coat color or pattern. Genotype is the set of alleles the rabbit carries. A rabbit can show a dominant phenotype while silently carrying a recessive allele, so two rabbits that look alike may not produce the same colors.

That is why breeders use pedigree information, parentage, previous offspring, and genetic testing where available to refine what is known.

Dominant and recessive does not mean better and worse

A dominant allele can influence phenotype when one copy is present. A recessive phenotype generally requires two recessive copies at that locus. Dominance describes expression, not quality, health, rarity, or value.

The A locus: pattern

The A locus helps control coat pattern. In the commonly taught rabbit color framework, A is associated with agouti, aᵗ with tan pattern, and a with self. A rabbit showing agouti may still carry a more recessive tan-pattern or self allele.

The B locus: black base versus chocolate base

The B locus is a simple place to learn. B is dominant and supports a black pigment base. The recessive bb genotype produces a chocolate base. A black-based rabbit can therefore be BB or Bb; the phenotype alone may not tell you which.

The C locus: how much pigment is expressed

The C series influences pigment expression and includes full-color, chinchilla-related, Himalayan, and albino forms in the commonly taught series. The important beginner concept is that one locus can mask what is happening at other loci. An albino rabbit can still carry color alleles that are invisible in its coat.

The D locus: dense versus dilute

D allows dense pigment while dd produces dilution. A black base can dilute to blue, and a chocolate base can dilute to lilac. A dense-colored rabbit may carry dilution without showing it.

The E locus: extension of dark pigment

The E locus helps control how dark pigment extends through the coat. It includes more than a simple two-allele dominant/recessive relationship and contributes to phenotypes such as normal extension, steel, harlequin or brindling, and non-extension red/orange/fawn patterns depending on the allele combination and the other loci present.

Why one Punnett square is not enough for the whole rabbit

A Punnett square can model one known locus well. Coat color, however, is the result of several loci interacting. One gene can modify or mask another, a process called epistasis. That is why a correct prediction starts with the loci you actually know and clearly marks what remains uncertain.

Use the underscore when you do not know

Breeders often use an underscore to show that one allele is unknown. B_ means the rabbit has at least one B allele but the second allele is not proven. Recording uncertainty is more useful than filling the blank with a guess.

Beginner workflow

  1. Record the rabbit’s phenotype using consistent breed terminology.
  2. Record only genotypes that are actually supported by phenotype, parents, offspring, or testing.
  3. Use an underscore or unknown status when an allele is not known.
  4. Work through one locus at a time before combining loci.
  5. Treat every unexpected kit as new evidence about the parents.
  6. Remember that coat color is only one part of a complete breeding decision.

Bottom line

Rabbit color genetics gets much easier when you stop trying to memorize every possible color and instead learn what each locus does. Track what is known, preserve uncertainty, and let offspring and records improve the genotype picture over time.
