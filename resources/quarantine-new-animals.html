Quarantine Protocols When Bringing a New Animal Into Your Herd or Rabbitry

Published October 7, 2026 · 3 min read · Beginner

In this guide: Quarantine reduces the chance that a new arrival introduces disease into an established group. It works only when separation includes animals, equipment, people, records, and a clear release decision.

Quarantine is a biosecurity tool

New animals can carry infectious disease before signs are obvious. Transport stress can also make health problems more apparent after arrival.

Quarantine creates time and separation to observe the animal and complete appropriate testing, preventive care, or treatment before contact with the resident group.

Use real separation

A quarantine pen that shares direct nose-to-nose contact, manure flow, tools, or constant traffic with the main group may provide little protection.

The appropriate distance and physical setup depend on species and disease risk, but the goal is to break transmission routes.

Separate equipment

Where practical, use dedicated:

  • feed and water containers,
  • cleaning tools,
  • grooming equipment,
  • transport carriers,
  • protective clothing or footwear.

If equipment must be shared, clean and disinfect it using a protocol appropriate to the organism and material.

Work from lower risk to higher risk

A common biosecurity principle is to care for established healthy animals before quarantined or sick animals, then clean up before returning to the resident group.

The exact workflow should fit the facility.

Observe and record

Create an arrival record including:

  • source,
  • date,
  • transport details,
  • identification,
  • health documents,
  • vaccination/testing history where applicable,
  • medications,
  • body condition,
  • baseline weight when useful.

During quarantine, record changes in appetite, feces, respiratory signs, skin/coat, movement, behavior, and other species-specific indicators.

Testing should match the risk

There is no universal test panel appropriate for every animal or operation.

Testing decisions depend on:

  • species,
  • source herd/flock/rabbitry,
  • geography,
  • disease prevalence,
  • age,
  • breeding purpose,
  • local regulations,
  • veterinarian recommendations.

Avoid ordering tests simply because another species or operation uses them.

Quarantine duration is not one-size-fits-all

Different diseases have different incubation periods, and different operations have different risk tolerance.

Set a quarantine period with species-appropriate veterinary or Extension guidance. Extending time does not compensate for poor separation or ignoring signs of illness.

Do not introduce a sick animal at the end of the calendar

Release should be based on health status and required testing—not merely because a target number of days has passed.

If the animal becomes ill, the quarantine plan should be reassessed.

Protect the quarantined animal too

Biosecurity works both ways. A new arrival may also be vulnerable to pathogens already present in the resident group.

Clean housing, low stress, appropriate nutrition, and careful acclimation matter.

Keep breeding separate until release

Do not bypass quarantine because the animal was purchased for a time-sensitive breeding.

A delayed mating is usually easier to manage than introducing a contagious disease into the entire breeding population.

Bottom line

Quarantine is not a spare cage or pen. It is a controlled process: separate, observe, test when appropriate, document, and release only when the animal meets the operation's health criteria.
