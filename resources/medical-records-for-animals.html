Keeping Accurate Medical Records for Livestock and Rabbits

Published October 7, 2026 · 3 min read · Beginner

In this guide: Medical records should tell the story of what was observed, what was diagnosed, what was done, and what happened afterward. Good records protect animal care and improve breeding decisions.

Record the observation before rewriting the story

Start with what was actually seen.

Examples:

  • reduced appetite,
  • lameness,
  • abnormal stool,
  • respiratory signs,
  • injury,
  • reproductive complication,
  • sudden weight change.

If a diagnosis is established later, add it. Do not replace the original observation as though the diagnosis was known from the beginning.

Core fields

For each health event, record:

  • animal ID,
  • date and time when relevant,
  • signs or reason for treatment,
  • temperature or measurements if taken,
  • veterinarian or clinic if involved,
  • diagnosis or differential when provided,
  • treatment,
  • product/medication,
  • dose, route, frequency, and duration as directed,
  • testing and results,
  • follow-up,
  • outcome.

Medication records require precision

Do not write "antibiotic" or "dewormer" if the actual product is known.

Product identity matters because concentration, dose, route, contraindications, and withdrawal requirements can differ.

For food-producing animals, follow the approved label withdrawal time for labeled use. If a veterinarian directs an extra-label use, the veterinarian must establish an appropriate withdrawal, withholding, or discard interval. Never reuse a withdrawal period from another product, species, dose, route, or treatment plan.

Keep preventive care separate but connected

Track preventive events such as:

  • vaccinations where applicable,
  • parasite-control plans,
  • testing programs,
  • hoof or dental work,
  • screening relevant to the species or herd,
  • biosecurity events.

That creates a useful timeline instead of scattering health information in unrelated notes.

Record recurring problems

One health event may be random. Repetition across the same animal or family may change the breeding decision.

A searchable history can reveal:

  • repeated reproductive problems,
  • recurring dental issues,
  • chronic foot problems,
  • repeated respiratory disease,
  • offspring with similar problems,
  • management problems affecting several animals at the same time.

Do not assume a repeated issue is genetic, but do not ignore the pattern either.

Preserve test results and provenance

If a laboratory test, imaging report, pathology result, or veterinary note exists, store the actual result when possible rather than only typing "negative" or "normal."

Record:

  • date,
  • test name,
  • laboratory/provider,
  • result,
  • specimen when relevant.

That preserves context if testing standards or interpretation change later.

Separate fact from breeder interpretation

A useful record may contain both:

Fact: "Doe failed to conceive after three documented matings."

Interpretation: "Possible declining fertility; reassess before next breeding."

Keeping those separate helps prevent opinion from being mistaken for a diagnosis.

Privacy and buyer information

Medical records may contain information that should not automatically become public.

A marketplace listing can disclose appropriate health information without exposing private veterinary documents, owner contact information, or internal notes.

The breeding record and the public listing do not need to be the same record.

Use health history in selection

When deciding whether to retain or breed an animal, review the health history alongside pedigree and performance.

A beautiful animal with repeated serious problems can create a long-term breeding cost that is easy to miss when records are scattered.

Bottom line

Good medical records are chronological, specific, and difficult to misinterpret. They help the current animal, the veterinarian, the breeder, and the next generation of breeding decisions.
