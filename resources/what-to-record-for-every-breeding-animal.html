What to Record for Every Animal in a Breeding Program

Published October 7, 2026 · Beginner · About 3 min read

Short version: If a record will change a future breeding, health, culling, or management decision, it belongs in the animal’s history. The strongest system connects identity, pedigree, reproduction, health, performance, offspring, and treatments in one place.

1. Permanent identity

Every record depends on knowing exactly which animal it belongs to. Use a unique permanent identifier appropriate to the species—ear number, tag, tattoo, microchip, registration number, or another durable system—and keep the barn name as a separate convenience field.

Record species, breed, sex, date of birth, breeder or source, ownership status, and clear photos where useful.

2. Parentage and pedigree

Record sire and dam with unique IDs, not names alone. Keep imported pedigree information connected to the actual animal record so ancestry, performance, and health history can be evaluated together.

Unknown parentage should remain unknown rather than being filled with a likely guess.

3. Breeding and reproductive history

  • Every breeding or exposure date and mate.
  • Pregnancy confirmation or failure to conceive when known.
  • Expected and actual birth or kindling dates.
  • Litter or offspring count, survival, and weaning outcomes.
  • Dystocia, assisted birth, retained placenta, poor mothering, or other reproductive complications where applicable.
  • Fertility history for males as well as females.

4. Health and medical history

Record illnesses, injuries, diagnoses, procedures, vaccinations where applicable, parasite treatments, medications, adverse reactions, and recurring problems. A vague note that an animal “was sick last spring” is much less useful than a dated record with symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, and outcome.

For food-producing animals, treatment records should also capture drug name, dose, route, administration dates, and withdrawal information when applicable. FDA specifically emphasizes treatment and withdrawal records to prevent illegal residues.

5. Growth and performance

Record measurements that matter to the species and your breeding objective: birth weight, weaning weight, mature weight, milk production, litter size, growth rate, egg production, fiber traits, feed efficiency, show evaluation, or other useful performance indicators.

Performance data is strongest when the measurement method and age or stage are consistent enough to compare animals fairly.

6. Structure and temperament

Written evaluation helps prevent memory from becoming selective. Record structural strengths and weaknesses, disposition, handling concerns, and any trait that affects function or suitability for your program.

A photo is helpful but should not replace the written observation, because angle, age, grooming, and condition can make photographs misleading.

7. Offspring results

Breeding animals should eventually be judged by what they produce. Link offspring to the parents and track which traits repeat across multiple matings. One excellent offspring can happen by chance; repeated results are much more informative.

For females, track maternal performance and longevity. For males, track the consistency and weaknesses seen across multiple dams or females.

8. Management events

Record transfers, sales, deaths, culls, retirements, quarantine periods, housing changes, and ownership changes. These events provide context when you later review health or performance patterns.

A useful rule for deciding what deserves a record

Ask: “Could this information change what I breed, keep, sell, treat, quarantine, or cull later?” If the answer is yes, record it. The goal is not to collect every possible detail; it is to preserve the details you will otherwise wish you had when a decision becomes important.

Bottom line

Good recordkeeping turns breeding from memory into evidence. The value is not the number of fields you fill out—it is the ability to connect ancestry, performance, reproduction, and health over time so better patterns become visible.
